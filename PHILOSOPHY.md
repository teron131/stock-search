### 1. The engine’s worldview

The first thing is the worldview it assumes about markets.

This engine does **not** believe in a fragile AI bubble that will pop and never return. It assumes we are in a **long structural bull market** driven by AI, automation and US mega-cap tech, very similar to the way Tom Lee talks about long secular bull runs in US equities.

In this worldview, large drawdowns are **part of the path**, not the end of the story, as long as certain conditions hold: earnings remain strong, margins stay healthy, credit conditions are not collapsing, and policy is not actively killing the sector. When those conditions hold and prices fall a lot, the engine interprets that as a **buying opportunity**, not a signal to exit.

So the engine’s job is not “predict the top” but “measure risk, track fundamentals, and tell you when a dip is justified to buy or when a thesis is actually broken”.

---

### 2. How it “sees” your portfolio

The engine does not see a random list of tickers. It sees a **deliberate structure**, roughly in four groups.

First, there are **core engines**. These are the big AI and tech names that you believe are the true engines of the AI supercycle: NVIDIA, Alphabet, maybe a few others like Broadcom or Amazon. They are allowed to be large and central. Their role is to drive most of your long-term compounding.

Second, there are **core satellites**. These are still strong companies with decent fundamentals and structural tailwinds, but not quite at the same level as the core engines in your mind. Here you place things like Tesla, certain memory or infrastructure names, and other “winners” that benefit from AI but are more cyclical or have more idiosyncratic risk. They support the story and diversify the drivers a bit.

Third, there is a **FOMO or high-beta bucket**. This is the fun but dangerous space: miners, high-IV AI plays, more speculative names. This bucket is intentionally small relative to your whole portfolio, and the point is to keep the risk controlled while still having some exposure to explosive upside.

Fourth, there is a **defensive or ballast sleeve**. Names like Berkshire, gold proxies, healthcare, and defense live here. Their purpose is not to beat NVIDIA, but to smooth the ride and reduce the psychological and financial pain during large AI drawdowns.

The methodology here is simple but important: every time you look at a holding, you mentally place it into one of these four roles. That classification then determines how big it is allowed to be, how aggressively you buy dips, and how quickly you cut it if things sour.

### 3. Delta-equivalent exposure and “how big is this really”

Because you use options heavily, especially long-dated calls, the engine cannot just look at “number of shares” or “premium paid”. It must think in **delta-equivalent exposure**.

For each position, the engine asks: “If this name moves by one unit of price, how much does my portfolio move?”

For plain shares, this is essentially one-to-one. For call options, it is the option’s sensitivity to the underlying price, which you normally think of as delta, times the number of units. Spreads can be reduced to the difference between the long and short legs. This gives a single number that says, “This is equivalent to holding this many shares.”

Once it has this equivalent share count, it multiplies by the current price to get an effective **notional exposure** for that name. Then it compares that exposure to the total equity you have in the account. This ratio is the **weight** that really matters to you.

Methodologically, this means that “how big” a position is in your system is not “how much cash I spent” but “how much of my portfolio’s behavior this name controls”. This matches how you already think in terms of delta-equivalent shares and explains why you allow huge LEAP positions in core engines but keep FOMO things small even if the premium is low.

### 4. Valuation and quality: PEG-first, but not PEG-only

On the valuation side, your engine is heavily biased toward **PEG** rather than raw P/E. You are comfortable with high P/E as long as growth justifies it. In your framework, a PEG below about 2 feels cheap in this era of fat margins and high returns on capital. A PEG below 1, when the business is high quality, feels like a serious bargain.

So for each company, the engine asks:

* What is the current earnings multiple?
* What is a realistic, not fantasy, expectation for growth over the relevant horizon?
* How does their ratio compare to your comfort band?

But valuation is never isolated. The engine also keeps a sense of **business quality**: revenue growth over years, profit margins, capital efficiency, and balance sheet strength. It blends these into a mental quality score.

The methodology here is that core engines are allowed to be richly valued if their quality is extremely high. Satellites need a slightly stronger valuation argument or a clearer optionality story. FOMO names are not held because they are cheap on PEG; they are held because of convex optionality and are constrained by the FOMO risk budget rather than valuation purity. Defensive names are held more for stability and diversification than for maximum PEG efficiency.

### 5. Thesis status and narrative layer

You are not a pure quant. Narrative and structural context matter a lot.

For each name you care about, there is a **thesis**: why this company is a structural winner in your AI/automation view, what you expect management to do, how you expect capex and strategy to evolve, and how it fits into the broader AI factory and robotics story.

Your engine should always carry a simple mental label for that thesis: intact, weakened, or broken.

Intact means the story is progressing roughly as expected. Weakend means there are warning signs: perhaps growth is slowing in a way that contradicts your prior expectations, or management signals worry you, even if the stock price has not yet collapsed. Broken means one of the core assumptions is no longer believable; for example, the company loses its technological edge, regulators cut its core profit engine, or capital allocation turns into clown mode.

Methodologically, this thesis status acts as a gatekeeper for every other decision. A deep price dip with an intact thesis is a potential buy. A deep price dip with a broken thesis is not an opportunity; it is a trap. Likewise, a strong rally with an intact thesis is a reason to review position size, not necessarily to dump everything. A strong rally with a weakened thesis might be the chance to reduce.

You can see why qualitative judgment and AI tools fit well here: models can help you summarize news, earnings, and commentary, but the final “intact/weak/broken” label is a human judgment your engine relies on.

### 6. Risk and stress testing: survival before perfection

Because you use leverage and options, survival is non-negotiable. The engine embodies a clear principle: **you must be able to live through brutal drawdowns without being forced out by margin calls or psychological collapse**.

To operationalize that, the engine imagines stress scenarios: for example, “What happens if all AI names drop 30–40% from here?” It then applies those drops to the effective notional exposures it computed earlier. If under such a shock your equity remains positive and comfortably above any margin requirements, then leverage is considered survivable. If not, the system is overextended.

This stress approach is applied both at portfolio level and at bucket level. The FOMO sleeve, in particular, is treated as “sacrificial”; if it goes to zero in a crash, your core engines must still be safe and intact. Defensive positions are the opposite: they should soften the blow in those scenarios.

The methodology is conservative: you never assume that you can sell at the perfect time or that volatility will be gentle. Instead, you plan for ugly but historically plausible drawdowns and size your risk so that you can stay in the game to enjoy the structural bull.

---

### 7. Decision logic: when to add, when to trim, when to roll

Once all the above pieces are in place, the engine’s behavior becomes clear and simple.

When a core or satellite name falls significantly from recent highs, the engine asks:

* Has the fundamental thesis remained intact?
* Is the valuation, especially PEG, now better than before?
* Is there room within your position size and bucket constraints to add more exposure without breaking your risk rules?

If the answers are yes, the methodology says this is a **buy-the-dip opportunity**, particularly for your core engines.

When a name runs very far above your prior expectations, the engine asks:

* Has the thesis improved so much that this new level is justified, or has sentiment simply stretched?
* Is the position now dominating your portfolio in terms of effective exposure?
* Are you comfortable with the stress scenarios given this new size?

If the position has become oversized relative to your rules, even with an intact thesis, the method suggests trimming to bring it back into a healthy range. You do not trim because “the top is in” but because the risk-reward balance in your personal system is out of line.

For options, there is a second timing axis: time to expiry. The methodology here is that long-dated calls are used as stock replacements with time protection. You do not want to hold them into the period where theta becomes aggressive and time decay dominates. So the engine watches the remaining time and, when it becomes too short for your comfort, it considers the position due for a **roll**: reducing or closing the old calls and shifting exposure to new calls with more time, preserving the structural view while avoiding the worst of the decay curve.

---

### 8. How all this fits together as a methodology

In words, your methodology looks like this:

You start from a **structural macro belief** in a long AI-led bull market, tempered by risk management inspired by Tom Lee’s way of looking at cycles and drawdowns. You then organize your holdings into a few conceptual roles: core engines, satellites, FOMO, and defensive ballast. You quantify every position in terms of its true sensitivity to price changes, not superficial cash cost, so that you know what actually drives your portfolio.

You overlay this with a valuation and quality view, centered on PEG and business fundamentals, and a narrative layer that tracks whether each company’s story is intact or broken. You then run stress-test style mental experiments to see whether your current leverage and bucket sizes allow you to survive big shocks without being liquidated or panicking.

Finally, you apply straightforward rules: buy dips in intact, high-quality names within your risk constraints; trim overgrown positions even if they are winning; roll options before time decay turns them into wasting assets; and refuse to treat every drop as a bargain if the underlying thesis has clearly failed.

That is the “understanding” you want to embed: not a mechanical trading system, but a consistent way of measuring exposure, judging fundamentals, and converting your structural beliefs into concrete, survivable decisions. Once this conceptual logic is clear, turning it into an automated analysis tool is mostly a matter of carefully translating each of these verbal checks into numerical checks and thresholds that match your taste.
